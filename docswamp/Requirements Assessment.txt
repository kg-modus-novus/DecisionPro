The committee does not really want “a dashboard.” It wants a decision-support system that helps legislators answer three questions:

Where is Medicaid money going?
What is driving the increase?
What policy or contractual action should be taken?

That distinction should drive the design.

What would make them want it

The dashboard becomes compelling when it lets a legislator move from a statewide warning signal to an understandable cause in under a minute.

For example:

Medicaid hospital spending increased 11.8% this year.
Most of the increase came from the aged and disabled population.
Three service categories account for 72% of the increase.
Two regions have unusually high avoidable admissions.
Readmissions and pharmacy costs are the largest controllable contributors.
Here are the existing MCO or provider measures related to those problems.

That is what they are looking for: not more data, but a credible explanation of what changed, why it changed, whom it affects, and where intervention is possible.

The transcript repeatedly emphasizes converting enormous volumes of Medicaid data into meaningful, manageable and actionable information, especially for legislative budgeting and policy decisions.

Core product requirements
1. The initial audience must be legislators

The committee ultimately discussed possible public-facing uses, but the first version should be designed specifically for:

Legislators
Legislative Research Commission analysts
Medicaid leadership
Budget and oversight staff

This means the dashboard should not begin as a consumer provider-selection tool.

The legislative version should emphasize:

Cost
Utilization
Outcomes
Variance
Trends
Accountability
Policy implications

A later public version can present simplified population-health and program-performance information.

2. Start with a constrained executive view

Several participants warned that Medicaid data is so large and complex that the project could become overwhelming. They explicitly recommended starting small, using existing measures and concentrating on cost drivers and managed-care metrics.

The landing page should therefore contain perhaps 10 to 15 top-level indicators, not hundreds.

A good first screen would include:

Total Medicaid expenditure
Year-over-year expenditure change
Per-member-per-month cost
Enrollment
Cost per enrollee
Hospital inpatient utilization
Emergency department utilization
Readmission rate
Pharmacy expenditure
Behavioral-health expenditure and utilization
Preventive-care performance
Maternal and child health performance
Adult well-visit rate
Avoidable or low-value care estimate
MCO quality-performance status

Every indicator should show:

Current value
Prior-period value
Percentage change
Target or benchmark
Direction of concern
Data freshness
Population chiefly responsible for the change
3. The primary organizing framework should be cost, utilization and outcomes

The committee discussed many possible categories, but their comments converge around three principal questions.

Cost
How much is being spent?
On which services?
For which populations?
In which regions?
Through which MCOs and providers?
What is driving the increase?
Utilization
What services are people using?
How often?
Are utilization levels expected for that population?
Are people using high-cost settings because lower-cost care is unavailable?
Are hospital days, emergency visits or readmissions increasing?
Outcomes
Is the program improving health?
Are preventive measures being completed?
Are maternal, child, chronic-disease and behavioral-health outcomes improving?
Are expenditures producing measurable value?

The dashboard should make these three lenses visually explicit.

The most important functional requirement: population segmentation

The committee was emphatic that aggregate Medicaid figures can be misleading.

At minimum, every major measure should be filterable by:

Children and CHIP
Expansion adults
Aged population
Disabled population
Pregnant and postpartum members
Traditional low-income adults
Managed-care versus fee-for-service populations

Without these categories, high utilization by aged and disabled beneficiaries could make the entire program appear inefficient, while low utilization in another population could conceal access problems.

This segmentation should not be buried in an advanced filter. It should be one of the dashboard’s primary controls.

A useful default presentation would show:

Measure	Overall	Children	Expansion	Aged	Disabled
Cost per member					
ED visits per 1,000					
Inpatient days per 1,000					
Readmission rate					

That immediately helps users see whether a statewide number reflects a broad problem or a concentrated one.

Recommended dashboard structure
Page 1: Legislative Command Center

This is the “what requires attention?” screen.

It should answer:

What changed?
Is it material?
Is it expected?
Which population or service caused it?
Is it improving or worsening?
Does it require investigation?

Recommended components:

Program snapshot
Total spend
Enrollment
Cost per member
State-budget exposure
Federal versus state funding share
Forecast versus actual
Top changes

A ranked list such as:

Pharmacy expenditure increased by $142 million.
Inpatient days among disabled beneficiaries increased 9%.
Emergency utilization increased in six rural counties.
Postpartum follow-up improved 4.7 percentage points.
One MCO missed three quality targets.
Attention panel

Indicators classified as:

On target
Watch
Intervention indicated
Data incomplete

Avoid decorative red-yellow-green scoring unless the thresholds are defensible and transparent.

Page 2: Cost Drivers

This is likely the page that would create the strongest immediate demand.

It should show:

Total spending by service category
Dollar contribution to annual growth
Cost per member by population
Cost concentration
Highest-growth categories
Pharmacy cost by therapeutic class
Inpatient and outpatient costs
Behavioral-health and substance-use costs
Long-term-care costs
MCO administrative and medical cost patterns, where available

The key visualization should not merely show the largest expenses. It should distinguish:

Largest current costs
Fastest-growing costs
Largest contributors to the dollar increase
Potentially controllable costs
Expected demographic or acuity-related costs

A $2 billion category growing 1% may deserve less attention than a $200 million category growing 30%.

Suggested measures:

The committee specifically raised geographic travel distance and rural access. The system should display where beneficiaries live versus where care is delivered, while accounting for provider billing addresses that differ from service locations.

Initial measure families should include:

The committee does not want activity measures that lack meaningful impact. Every measure should therefore be labeled by type:

That will help distinguish “we performed more screenings” from “disease was detected earlier” or “hospitalization declined.”

Page 5: MCO Accountability

The transcript clearly connects the dashboard to managed-care oversight and possible future contract measures.

This page should include:

MCO enrollment
MCO spending and utilization patterns
Current value-based payment measures
Performance against the 2% quality withholding
Amount earned back
Measures missed
Trend over time
Comparison among MCOs
Complaint and grievance information, if available
Prior authorization or access indicators, if available
Network adequacy indicators
Encounter-data completeness
Performance by population and region

The dashboard should distinguish between:

Existing contractual measures
Measures being monitored but not contracted
Candidate future contractual measures

That turns the product into a practical contracting tool without making the first version dependent on contract redesign.

Page 6: Provider and Delivery-System Performance

The committee discussed university-directed payment programs and existing provider metrics as a possible proof of concept.

The page should show:

Hospital utilization and outcomes
Readmissions
Preventive-care follow-up
State-directed payment measures
High-value versus low-value care
Performance adjusted for population mix
Geographic and social-risk context

Provider comparisons must include contextual safeguards. The committee specifically noted that readmissions or poor outcomes may be affected by housing, food, heat, transportation and other social conditions, not simply provider quality.

The dashboard should therefore show both:

Unadjusted performance
Contextualized or risk-adjusted performance
Page 7: County and Regional Health View

This page should allow users to select a county or legislative district and see:

Medicaid enrollment
Expenditure
Cost per member
Major service categories
ED utilization
Hospitalization
Preventive care
Maternal and child measures
Behavioral health
Distance to care
Provider availability
Social determinants
Comparison with state and peer counties

A legislative-district filter would make the dashboard particularly desirable to legislators. Each member could immediately understand what is happening in the population they represent.

Page 8: Benchmarks

The committee asked what Kentucky performance should be compared against.

The dashboard should support several benchmark types:

Kentucky historical performance
State target
National Medicaid average
Peer states
Best-performing comparable states
CMS Medicaid and CHIP Scorecard
HEDIS benchmarks
Contract targets
Statutory or policy goals

A single benchmark will not work for every measure.

Users should be able to see both absolute performance and relative position.

The feature that will separate this from an ordinary dashboard
“Explain this change”

Every chart or indicator should have an explanation layer.

When a user selects a measure, the system should generate a structured explanation such as:

What changed

Emergency department visits rose 7.2% during the last four quarters.

Where it changed

The increase was concentrated in eight eastern Kentucky counties.

Who was affected

Expansion adults accounted for 61% of the increase.

What contributed

Behavioral-health and respiratory diagnoses were the largest contributors.

Why it may matter

Primary-care access and behavioral-health follow-up rates declined in the same counties.

What should be examined

MCO network adequacy
Primary-care availability
Behavioral-health follow-up
Transportation barriers

This can be rules-based initially. It does not require speculative artificial intelligence.

The key is that the dashboard should explain the relationship between indicators rather than forcing the legislator to inspect several unrelated charts.

Actionability requirements

Each important measure should include a “What can be done?” section.

Possible action categories:

Legislative policy
Budget allocation
MCO contracting
Provider incentive
Public-health intervention
Data-quality remediation
Further investigation

For example:

Finding	Possible action
Rising avoidable ED use	Review primary-care access and MCO network adequacy
Poor postpartum follow-up	Add or revise value-based maternal-health measure
Rapid pharmacy-cost increase	Examine therapeutic class, rebate and prescribing patterns
High rural travel distance	Assess local provider capacity and transportation support
Readmission concentration	Review discharge follow-up and social-risk conditions

The dashboard should not prescribe policy as fact. It should identify plausible intervention points and the evidence supporting them.

Data and governance requirements
Single source of truth

Participants repeatedly stressed confidence in the numbers. The dashboard should have an authoritative data layer, even when information originates in multiple systems.

Every measure needs:

Named data owner
Source system
Calculation definition
Refresh cadence
Last refresh date
Coverage period
Known limitations
Revision history
Suppression rules
Responsible analyst or office

The visible dashboard can federate multiple systems, but there must be a governed semantic layer that defines what each number means.

Data inventory before full build

The committee identified more than 200 possible datasets and several existing repositories.

A formal data catalogue should document:

Dataset
Owning agency
Subject area
Population covered
Time range
Refresh frequency
Grain
Identifiers
Privacy classification
Data-sharing restrictions
Known quality issues
Candidate dashboard measures

This inventory is not merely an IT exercise. It will prevent duplicate metrics and conflicting versions of the same measure.

Use existing measures first

The first release should begin with established sources such as:

CMS Medicaid and CHIP Scorecard
CMS adult and child core measures
HEDIS
Current MCO value-based payment measures
State-directed payment measures
Existing Office of Health Data and Analytics reporting
Claims
Eligibility
Provider enrollment
Vital statistics
Public-health data

This gives the dashboard credibility and reduces debates about definitions.

Data freshness

Users must see both:

Service or measurement period
Actual publication or refresh date

The committee noted that some national comparison data may lag by several years. A measure dated “2024” should not appear beside near-current claims data without a freshness warning.

Recommended freshness labels:

Near current
Recent
Lagged
Historical
Provisional
Privacy and security requirements

The legislative dashboard should normally use aggregated and de-identified information.

Necessary controls include:

Minimum cell-size suppression
Role-based access
Public versus internal views
Audit logs
Download restrictions
HIPAA and Medicaid-data-use compliance
Data-sharing agreements
No person-level identification in legislative views
Additional restrictions for behavioral-health or substance-use data where applicable

The dashboard should be built so that the public version is generated from approved aggregated datasets, not by simply removing a login from the internal version.

What not to build

They will not value a dashboard that is:

A wall of charts
A catalog of all available Medicaid data
Primarily a public-relations scorecard
Limited to statewide averages
Based only on quality measures without costs
Based only on cost without outcomes
Unable to segment populations
Filled with data that is two or three years old without warning
Unable to explain metric definitions
Unable to reconcile conflicting sources
Designed before the audience and policy questions are settled

The strongest positioning statement

The dashboard should be presented to them as:

A legislative Medicaid decision system that shows where spending is changing, what populations and services are driving it, whether health outcomes are improving, and where policy, contracting or program intervention may be warranted.

That will make them want it more than describing it as a “health transparency dashboard.”

Transparency is the mechanism.

Legislative control, credible oversight and actionable insight are the value proposition.
